The Co-op-Off Playbook

The seller listed their home and offered to pay a buyer's agent ~2.5%. If you show up without one, that money can become yours — negotiated into the price or a closing credit. Here's the whole play: the numbers, the exact words, the paperwork, and where it goes wrong.

1 · The play, in one paragraph

In Ontario you can't collect the commission yourself (TRESA) — remuneration is paid under the seller's listing agreement. What you can do: show up unrepresented and ask the seller to reflect the value as a price reduction or a credit at closing. It's a negotiation, not a right — some sellers play, some don't. This page is how to run it properly.

2 · Before you make the offer

  • Run your numbers. The Co-op-Off calculator shows what the co-op is worth at this price — walk in with the figure.
  • Ask the listing agent, in writing: "What remuneration is being offered to a buyer's agent, and what happens to it if the buyer is unrepresented?" You're entitled to a truthful answer.
  • Be a strong buyer on paper. Pre-approval in hand, deposit ready, inspection plan — your offer must stand on its own without an agent carrying you.
  • Decide your walk-away and your fallback. Fallback = flat-fee licensed representation with the co-op rebated back to you (see the Fee Card).

3 · The ask — words that work

Send this to the listing agent, adjusted to your deal:

"Hi — we're buying without a buyer's agent. The listing offers X% to a buyer's agent, and since there's no agent on our side to pay, we're asking that value be reflected in the deal — either as a reduction of the purchase price or a seller credit at closing. Our offer is $Y and already accounts for that. We're pre-approved, flexible on the closing date, and can move quickly. Can we make that work?"

4 · The three answers you'll get

  • "Yes" as a price cut — the saving is locked into your mortgage from day one. The cleanest version.
  • "Yes" as a credit — same value, paid at closing (closing costs, adjustments). Structure differs, dollars don't.
  • "No." — happens. Then pick: accept as-is, take the flat-fee rebate route, or walk. Walking is a legitimate answer. That's what the calculator's slider is for.

5 · The paperwork reality

  • You'll be self-represented — no agency on your side. That's allowed; the listing brokerage always represents the seller, and everyone knows their side of the table.
  • You can still view the home (the listing agent shows it), submit offers, and negotiate directly.
  • Use a real-estate lawyer for review and closing — fixed fee, and the cheapest insurance you'll ever buy. (We're building a fixed-fee network for exactly this.)
  • Deposit, conditions, closing mechanics: same as any deal. The difference is only who's on your side of the table — and this time, it's you.

6 · Where this goes wrong (honest list)

  • Competing offers. In a bidding war, sellers rarely reduce anything. Run the play in normal markets, not feeding frenzies.
  • Sellers who won't engage. Some ignore the ask entirely. Your fallback exists for them.
  • The discipline problem. The biggest risk is you — falling for the house and overpaying to win it. The Leverage Scorer exists to keep you honest.
  • Skipping protections. Never drop inspection or financing conditions on someone's verbal promise. Conditions protect unrepresented buyers most of all.

7 · The checklist

1 · Run the numbers. 2 · Ask the co-op question in writing. 3 · Pre-approval + deposit ready. 4 · Make the offer with the ask built in. 5 · Negotiate — price or credit, your call. 6 · Lawyer reviews before you sign. 7 · Close. Keep the savings.

Sources & honest fine print

  • TRESA (Ontario, since Dec 2023): brokerage remuneration is paid under the seller's listing agreement; self-represented buyers cannot collect it. The price/credit ask is a negotiation with the seller.
  • Buyer-agent co-op commonly ~2.5% — always confirm what this listing offers, in writing.
